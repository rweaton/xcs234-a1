  \begin{itemize}
    \item When starting in state $s = 3$, the sequence of actions that the agent takes under the optimal
    policy is $(sell, 3 \rightarrow 2, +1)$, $(sell, 2 \rightarrow 1, +1)$, $(sell, 1 \rightarrow 0, +1)$,

    consequence of a myopic agent by setting $\gamma = 0$.

    \item For the scenario starting at $s = 9$, the execution sequence under the optimal policy migrates
    to the edge of the state space, in this case $s = 10$ --the terminal state--due to the inability of the agent 
    to include non-local possibilities in calculations of expected future return.  The complete execution sequence in 
    this case is: $(buy, 9 \rightarrow 10, +100)$, $(sell, 10 \rightarrow 10, 0)$.

\begin{answer}

  In the infinite-horizon scenario with reward discounting, it {\bf is} possible 
  to choose a fixed value $\gamma \in \left[0, 1\right)$ such that the optimal 
  policy never fully stocks the inventory. Setting $\gamma$ to a value for which 
  the expected cumulative discounted return for selling surpasses that for buying at
  state $s = 3$ would result in the inequality 
  \begin{center}
  $$100 \cdot \gamma^{6} < 1 + \gamma \cdot 1 + \gamma^2 \cdot 1$$,
  \end{center}
  with the quantity on the left being the expected discounted future return if the agent acted to $buy$ at 
  state $s = 3$, and the quantity on the right being the expected discounted future return if the
  agent chose to $sell$ at that state.

  The numerical estimate of the real positive root for the above polynomial is $\gamma < 0.5106$.
  Testing about this transition $\gamma$ value under value iteration with tolerance $1e^{-3}$, we find that
  the action selected at state $ = 3$ with discount factor set to $\gamma = 0.52$ is $buy$--from which the 
  sequence would end with the inventory fully stocked. Setting $\gamma = 0.51$, just under the theoretical 
  value, results in selected action $sell$ at state $s = 3$ whereby the agent's policy would 
  never fully stock the inventory at the end of the day.
  % ### END CODE HERE ###
\end{answer}

\begin{answer}
  % ### START CODE HERE ###
  To answer this question, we'll consider a scenario in which three human-operated cars (labeled by $H$) are traveling at
  the same speed along a roadway about to intercept an on-ramp on which is a single autonomous vehicle (labelled by $A$).
  We will consider a constant distance $d$ spanning the mergepoint of the onramp onto the main roadway to another endpoint,
  say 1/4 mile farther down the road, and that all four vehicles will be traveling that stretch of road together without 
  any of them turning off. The event of the autonomous vehicle merging will be labeled by $M$ and when it is stopped on the
  on-ramp by $S$.

  If the autonomous car merges onto the main roadway with velocity $v_{A,M}$ ahead of the other three human-operated 
  cars that were traveling at $v_{H,S} > v_{A,M}$ prior to the merge event, then the human operators must reduce their 
  speed to that of the autonomous vehicle to prevent collision.  In which case $v_{H, M} = v_{A, M}$.  So for the merge event
  mean velocity and average time of travel over distance $d$ are respectively
  \begin{center}
    $$ \bar{v}_{M} = \frac{1}{4} (3 v_{H,M} + v_{A,M}) = v_{A,M} $$
    $$ \bar{t}_{M} = \frac{d}{4} (\frac{3}{v_{H,M}} + \frac{1}{v_{A,M}}) = \frac{d}{v_{A,M}} $$
  \end{center}

  If the autonomous car is not allowed to merge so that the car's average velocity $v_{A,S} \rightarrow 0$, then correspondingly,
  its average time of travel over distance $d$ becomes $t_{A,S} \rightarrow \infty$.  In this case of no merge event by 
  the autonomous vehicle, we have
  \begin{center}
    $$ \bar{v}_{S} = \lim_{v_{A,S} \to 0} \frac{1}{4} (3 v_{H,S} + v_{A,S}) = \frac{3}{4} v_{H,S}$$
    $$ \bar{t}_{S} = \lim_{v_{A,S} \to 0} \frac{d}{4} (3 \frac{1}{v_{H,S}} + \frac{1}{v_{A,S}}) \approx \frac{d}{4 v_{A,S}} \rightarrow \infty $$
  \end{center}

  So, if $v_{H,S} > \frac{4}{3} v_{H,M}$ then $\bar{v}_{S} > \bar{v}_{M}$, that is, the average velocity of all four cars increases
  even though the autonomous car remains motionless on the merge on-ramp. Notice that the average time of travel over distance $d$ in
  this case $\bar{t}_{S}$ is much greater than the average time when the autonomous car performs the merge $\bar{t}_{M}$ since
  \begin{center}
    $$ \bar{t}_{M} = \frac{d}{v_{A,M}} \ll \lim_{v_{A,S} \to 0} \frac{d}{4 v_{A,S}} = \bar{t}_{S} $$
  \end{center}

  Therefore, by using mean velocity as a proxy reward function in place of commute time in the above-described scenario 
  (with $v_{H,S} > \frac{4}{3} v_{H,M}$), maximum mean velocity for the four cars is achieved by keeping the autonomous car stopped on the 
  on-ramp as its merging would otherwise impede the velocity of the arterial cars and reduce the mean velocity for the entire group.
  % ### END CODE HERE ###
\end{answer}

\begin{answer}
  % ### START CODE HERE ###
  Without optimizing for commute (time) which has the requirement that origin, destination, etc. of all drivers be known
  \textit{a prioi}, an alternative proxy reward function could be to return rewards on desirable changes in shape of the velocity
  distribution of all cars on the route. Returning rewards for positive shifts in median, reductions in variance
  and reduction of distribution weight in the near zero velocity domain could reveal policies that encourage more cars to travel
  at more uniform speeds. Unlike above, in which the reward function depended on a single summary statistic (e.g. the mean) that
  effectively promoted faster moving cars staying fast at the expense of the slower minority effectively made
  immobile, distribution shaping policies permit more detailed control over how potential policies can evolve by considering
  multiple desirable attributes in parallel.
  % ### END CODE HERE ###
\end{answer}
